Q1: True or False (4 marks)

1The post-architecture-stage model in the COCOMO family assumes that requirements are stable and the basic architecture of the software can be established after detailed design and coding.Show answer

False. "Requirements are stable and the basic architecture is established" describes the early design stage model. The post-architecture model is used during construction of the software.

2One of the primary responsibilities of a project manager is to ensure that adequate resources are available and managed effectively to complete the project successfully.Show answer

True.

3Activity cost estimates are quantitative assessments, not qualitative. They provide specific cost estimates for each activity or work package in the project.Show answer

True. They are numbers (costs) for each activity.

4LOC-based estimation techniques decompose the problem into software functions to estimate the required size and effort.Show answer

True. LOC estimation breaks the problem into software functions and estimates LOC for each. Compare MCQ 14: FP estimation decomposes by information domain values. This pair is easy to mix up.

5Empirical estimation models use future project data and regression analysis to derive estimation parameters and coefficients.Show answer

False. They use historical (past) project data, not future data. The word "future" is the trap.

6When deciding the type of software project, factors such as technical complexity, budget, and project goals are considered. The schedule to complete is more related to project planning rather than project type determination.Show answer

True. This matches MCQ 13: the schedule is not needed to decide the project type.

7The effort is determined as a function of program estimate, and a set of cost drivers is given for every phase of the software lifecycle.Show answer

True. This is the definition of Detailed COCOMO: cost drivers applied to each phase.

8Empirical estimation models are typically based on trial-and-error determination of the parameters and coefficients.Show answer

False. The parameters come from statistical analysis (regression) of historical data, not trial and error.

Effort, cost per unit, total cost

Revision sheet · Ch 26/33 · Lect 1

The sheet asks for (a) effort, (b) cost per LOC or per FP, (c) total project cost. You only need three formulas, and they work the same way for LOC and FP:

(a) Effort (person-months) = Size ÷ Productivity (b) Cost per unit = Labour rate ÷ Productivity (c) Total cost = Effort × Labour rate (= Size × Cost per unit)

Size is in LOC (or KLOC) or in FP. Productivity is LOC/pm or FP/pm. The labour rate is $ per person-month ("burdened" means it already includes overhead).

Sheet exampleSize = 36,000 LOC; productivity = 600 LOC/pm; burdened labour rate = $9,000/pm. Find (a) effort, (b) cost per LOC, (c) total cost.Show solution
(a) 36,000 ÷ 600 = 60 person-months (b) $9,000 ÷ 600 = $15 per LOC (c) 60 × $9,000 = $540,000 (check: 36,000 × $15 = $540,000)

Why project management is important

Lect 1 · Ch 31
  • It helps a company get the most organization and production for its money, and finish work on time as cheaply as possible.
  • It is the application of skills and knowledge that lets a company stay competitive in its market.
  • It has five categories: initiation, planning, execution, controlling, closing.
  • The project manager runs every aspect (scheduling, innovation, reaching the completion date), develops the strategy to organize and lead people and meet deadlines, and handles problems so executives can focus elsewhere.

The four P's (Ch 31)

  • People: the most important element of a successful project
  • Product: the software to be built
  • Process: the framework activities and tasks to get the job done
  • Project: all the work required to make the product a reality

Stakeholders

  • Senior managers (business issues)
  • Project (technical) managers
  • Practitioners (technical skills)
  • Customers (specify requirements)
  • End-users (use the released software)

Team leader: MOI model

  • Motivation: encourage people to do their best
  • Organization: mold or invent processes to turn the concept into a product
  • Ideas / innovation: help people feel creative within the product's bounds

SCM process tasks (Lect 1)

Identification of objects · Version control · Change control · Configuration audit · Status reporting.

SDLC (Software Development Life Cycle)

Lect 1

Why organizations use it

  1. A structured, organized approach to development
  2. High-quality software (testing and QA phases)
  3. Managing risks and costs early
  4. Better communication and collaboration with all stakeholders
  5. Better efficiency and productivity
  6. A higher chance of a successful project

The six phases, in order

  1. Requirements gathering and analysis: collect requirements from customers, end-users and business analysts.
  2. Design: architecture, data structures, interfaces. HLD (high-level design) gives the architecture; LLD (low-level design) describes how each feature and component works.
  3. Implementation / coding ("development"): the longest phase. Front end + middleware (connects front and back) + back end (database).
  4. Testing: confirm it meets requirements and works correctly.
  5. Deployment: release to production for end-users.
  6. Maintenance: ongoing support, bug fixes, updates.

Methodologies that implement the SDLC: Waterfall, Agile, Scrum, V-Model, DevOps.

Lines of Code (LOC)

Lect 1 · Ch 32

Definition: a line of code is any line that is not a comment or blank line, including header lines, however many statements are on it. It includes variable declarations and executable and non-executable statements. Because LOC only counts volume, use it only to compare projects in the same language with the same coding standards.

Features

  • Change tracking (growth or shrinkage over time)
  • Limited representation of complexity
  • Easy to compute
  • Easy to understand, even for non-technical people

Advantages

  • Effort estimation (high level)
  • Comparative analysis between projects or teams
  • Benchmarking between versions of the same program

Disadvantages

  • Poor fit for Agile, where requirements change
  • Ignores external libraries and frameworks
  • Larger codebases need more maintenance
  • Language-dependent; penalizes short, well-written code
  • Can't be known accurately until the code is written

Research shows a rough correlation between LOC and cost, duration and number of defects, so lower LOC is usually better.

Lect 1The selSort function is 13 lines in total, one of which is a comment. How many LOC?Show solution

Function Point (FP) analysis

Lect 1 · Ch 26/32

FPA measures software size and complexity from its functionality, from the user's point of view. It was developed by Allan J. Albrecht at IBM in 1979 and later refined by IFPUG (1984). It is language- and technology-independent and is mainly used for business systems and MIS.

The five information domain values, and their weights

ParameterExampleTypeSimpleAverageComplex
External Inputs (EI)Input screens, tablesTransactional346

A trick to memorize the average column: 4 · 5 · 4 · 10 · 7 (I · O · E · F · N). These are the weights used in almost every exercise.

The formula

UFP (count-total) = Σ (count × weight) CAF (= VAF = TCF) = 0.65 + 0.01 × Σ Fi FP = UFP × CAF
  • Σ Fi is the sum of 14 general system characteristics (GSCs), each rated 0 to 5 (0 = not applicable, 5 = essential).
  • So Σ Fi ranges 0 to 70 and CAF ranges 0.65 to 1.35.
  • Your files use three names for the same factor: CAF, VAF and TCF. The total-degree-of-influence version is VAF = TDI × 0.01 + 0.65.
  • When the count is given as Optimistic / Realistic / Pessimistic: estimated count = (O + 4R + P) ÷ 6, then multiply by the weight.

Cost and software estimation

Ch 26 / Ch 33 (same slides)

Goal of project planning: establish a pragmatic strategy for controlling, tracking and monitoring a complex technical project, so the result gets done on time and with quality.

Planning task set I

  • Establish project scope
  • Determine feasibility
  • Analyze risks
  • Define required resources (human, reusable software, environmental)

Planning task set II

  • Estimate cost and effort: decompose the problem, make two or more estimates (size, FP, process tasks or use-cases), reconcile them
  • Develop a project schedule

Estimation requires

Experience · access to good historical information (metrics) · the courage to commit to quantitative predictions when only qualitative information exists. Estimation carries inherent risk, and that risk leads to uncertainty.

Rules for project estimation

  • Scope must be understood
  • Elaboration (decomposition) is necessary
  • Historical metrics are very helpful
  • Use at least two different techniques
  • Uncertainty is inherent

Estimation techniques

  • Past (similar) project experience
  • Conventional: task breakdown and effort estimates; size (FP) estimates
  • Empirical models (for example COCOMO)
  • Automated tools

Estimation accuracy depends on

  • How well the planner estimated the size of the product
  • The ability to translate size into effort, calendar time and dollars (needs reliable metrics from past projects)
  • How well the plan reflects the team's abilities
  • The stability of requirements and of the environment

Feasibility (Ch 33)

  • Technical: hardware cost (system units, monitors, printers…) plus software cost (OS, server, front end, back end).
  • Operational: the cost of running the system at all sites (terminals, network cabling, operator salaries, backup).
  • Schedule: scheduled time vs activities, shown with a Gantt chart (horizontal bars over time, shows overlap) or a PERT chart (Program Evaluation and Review Technique, also shows dependencies between activities).

Cost–benefit analysis sheet: placed at the end of the proposal to show stakeholders that the tangible benefits will recover the investment cost.

COCOMO I (Basic): the calculation on the revision sheet

COCOMO (COnstructive COst MOdel) was published by Barry Boehm in 1981. It estimates effort from size in KLOC using one of three project modes.

Effort E = a × (KLOC)^b person-months Time D = c × (E)^d months Staff P = E ÷ D people Productivity = KLOC ÷ E
ModeWhenabcd

COCOMO II: Application Composition model (stage 1)

COCOMO II is a hierarchy of three models:

  • Application composition: early stages, when prototyping user interfaces, considering software and system interaction, assessing performance and evaluating technology maturity matter most.
  • Early design stage: once requirements are stable and the basic architecture is in place.
  • Post-architecture stage: during construction of the software.

Application composition sizes the system in object points: screens, reports and 3GL components.

  1. Count the screens, reports and 3GL components.
  2. Classify each one as simple, medium or difficult (tables below).
  3. Give each one a weight.
  4. Object points = Σ (instances × weight).
  5. NOP = object points × (100 − %reuse) ÷ 100.
  6. Look up PROD from developer experience.
  7. Effort = NOP ÷ PROD (person-months).

Defect Removal Efficiency (DRE)

Exercise docx · Ch 32
DRE = E ÷ (E + D) E = errors found BEFORE delivery to the end-user D = defects found AFTER delivery

A higher DRE is better; the ideal is 1 (100%). DRE is a quality-related process metric.

Ex 1180 errors before delivery, 20 defects after. Find DRE.Show solution
DRE = 180 ÷ (180 + 20) = 180 ÷ 200 = 0.90 = 90%

90% of defects were removed before delivery.

DRE = 240 ÷ 300 = 0.80 = 80% (20% escaped to the customer)

FP vs LOC

Lect 1 · Ch 32 · your comparison table

These four are the short, standard answer. Learn them first for a "compare FP and LOC" question, then add rows from the longer table below if more are asked.

FPLOC
★FP is specification-based.LOC is analogy-based.
★FP is language independent.LOC is language dependent.
★FP is user-oriented.LOC is design-oriented.
★It is extendible to LOC.It is convertible to FP (backfiring).

What the first and third rows mean: FP is counted from the requirements specification, from what the user sees. LOC is estimated by comparing with similar past code, and depends on how the program is designed and written.

Both measure the size of software and can be converted into each other with backfiring (LOC = FP × SLOC/FP).

Why use FP (Ch 32): it is independent of programming language; it uses readily countable characteristics determined early in the process; it does not "penalize" inventive (short) implementations; and it makes the impact of reusable components easier to measure.

Scope and software scope

Ch 26/33 · Ch 31

Software scope describes

  • The functions and features delivered to end-users
  • The data that is input and output
  • The "content" presented to users as a result of using the software
  • The performance, constraints, interfaces and reliability that bound the system

Scope is defined with one of two techniques: (1) a narrative description written after communicating with all stakeholders, or (2) a set of use-cases developed by end-users.

To understand scope

  • Understand the customer's needs
  • Understand the business context
  • Understand the project boundaries
  • Understand the customer's motivation
  • Understand the likely paths for change
  • Even when you understand, nothing is guaranteed

Product scope (Ch 31)

  • Context: how the software fits a larger system or business, and the constraints that follow
  • Information objectives: which customer-visible data objects are input and output
  • Function and performance: how input is transformed to output, and any special performance needs

Scope must be unambiguous and understandable at both management and technical levels.

Problem decomposition (partitioning, problem elaboration): once scope is defined, break it into functions, user-visible data objects or problem classes, and keep going until all are defined. Estimation depends on this step.

Baseline plan / PSD (Project Scope Document) lists the objectives, requirements, boundaries, deliverables, resources and constraints. Its benefits: it uncovers the main functional requirements, keeps the project tracking in the right direction, removes misunderstandings, keeps a feedback loop between customer, analyst and developer, and makes it easy to trace the effect of scope changes.

Lower priority, but they could appear in short-answer questions.

  • Team paradigms (Constantine): closed (hierarchy), random (individual initiative), open (control plus innovation), synchronous (compartmentalized, little communication). Agile teams are self-organizing.
  • Team toxicity: frenzied atmosphere, high frustration, fragmented procedures, unclear roles, repeated failure.
  • Why projects get into trouble: customer needs not understood, poorly defined scope, poorly managed change, technology changes, unrealistic deadlines, resistant users, lost sponsorship, missing skills.
  • Boehm's W5HH: Why? What? When? Who? Where? How? How much?
  • Metric: a quantitative measure of a characteristic of a system, process or project (for example defect density = defects ÷ KLOC). Process metrics are strategic; project metrics are tactical (inputs, outputs, results). Never use metrics to appraise or threaten individuals.
  • Quality measures: correctness, maintainability, integrity, usability.
  • CMMI: a process meta-model, either continuous (each process area improved separately) or staged (maturity levels). Each process area has specific goals (what to achieve) and specific practices (how).
  • People CMM levels: Initial → Managed → Defined → Predictable → Optimized.
  • SPI ROI = [Σ benefits − Σ costs] ÷ Σ costs × 100%. Other frameworks: SPICE (ISO/IEC 15504), Bootstrap, PSP/TSP, TickIT (ISO 9001).
